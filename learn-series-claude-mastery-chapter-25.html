Documentation & Knowledge Management

Write It Down Once, Keep It Useful Forever

⏱️ 12 min read ◆ 📊 Advanced ◆ 🎯 Knowledge Management

Almost every team has the same quiet problem: the most important knowledge lives in someone's head. How the monthly report is really built. Which workaround keeps the system running. Why a decision was made two years ago. When that person goes on leave or changes roles, the knowledge goes with them — and a stale document is arguably worse than none, because people trust it and follow instructions that no longer work.

Quick Answer

Documentation with Claude means feeding it raw material — meeting notes, chat threads, expert interviews — and getting back clean procedures, an organized knowledge base, and update-ready revisions. The human job shifts from writing to deciding what matters and checking that it is true: instruct Claude to flag unclear points instead of guessing, define the reader before writing, and give every document an owner and a review date so it does not quietly go stale.

Claude changes the economics of this work. It turns messy raw material into clean documentation in minutes, restructures what already exists, and helps keep it current. This chapter follows that lifecycle in four stages.

1 How Do You Capture Knowledge From Messy Sources?

Most useful knowledge never starts as a document. It starts as a meeting, a chat thread, a voice note, or a quick explanation to a new colleague. The job is not to write from a blank page but to feed Claude the raw material and shape what comes back.

From Raw Notes to Clean Documentation

Here are my rough notes from a call where a colleague explained how we handle customer refund requests. Turn them into a clear internal document. Keep every step and exception she mentioned, don't invent anything that isn't in the notes, and list any places where the notes were unclear or contradictory so I can confirm them with her.
Key Insight

Two instructions do most of the work. "Don't invent anything" protects accuracy, and "list where the notes were unclear" turns gaps into a short follow-up list instead of silent guesses.

Interviewing the Expert

When the knowledge lives in one person's head, ask Claude to run the interview.

I'm going to explain how our month-end reporting process works. Ask me questions one at a time, focusing on steps I might skip because they feel obvious to me, exceptions that cause problems, and what a new person would most likely get wrong. When we're done, write it up as a procedure.

Experts routinely skip the steps that feel obvious to them, and those are exactly the steps a newcomer needs. A patient interviewer surfaces them.

2 How Do You Write a Procedure Someone Else Can Follow?

The real test of a procedure is whether a person who has never done the task can complete it without asking a question.

Define the Audience First

The same process needs different documentation for different readers.

Write this procedure for someone in their first week who has system access but no background on our process. Assume they know nothing about our internal terminology, and define any term the first time it appears.

A Reliable Procedure Structure

Asking Claude to use a consistent format means every guide in the knowledge base feels familiar.

1
Purpose
One sentence on what this procedure achieves
2
When to use this
The situations that call for it
3
Prerequisites
Access, tools, and information needed before starting
4
Numbered steps
One action per step
5
Expected result
What the reader should see after each step — the piece most documentation skips
6
Common problems and fixes
What usually goes wrong and how to recover
7
Who to contact
The named person or team if the reader is stuck
Structure this as: Purpose (one sentence), When to use this, Prerequisites, Numbered steps (one action per step), Expected result for each step, Common problems and fixes, and Who to contact if stuck.

The expected result for each step lets the reader confirm they are on track instead of discovering a mistake five steps later.

Test It Before You Publish

Read this procedure as a first-time user. Identify any step where you'd have to guess, any term that isn't defined, and any place where a step assumes something that hasn't been explained.
💡

Why the check matters: It catches the gaps that the author, who knows the process too well, can no longer see.

3 How Do You Build a Knowledge Base People Can Navigate?

A pile of individual documents is not a knowledge base. If people can't find the right document in under a minute, they stop looking and ask a colleague instead — which is the exact problem documentation was meant to solve.

Designing the Structure

Here is a list of our current documents and their titles. Propose a simple folder structure with no more than six top-level categories, a consistent naming convention, and flag any documents that overlap or contradict each other.

Keep the structure shallow. Deep hierarchies hide things.

Summaries and Findability

For each of these documents, write a two-sentence summary and suggest five search keywords someone might type when looking for it, including the informal terms people actually use rather than the official title.

That last point matters more than it sounds. People search with the words they use in conversation, not the words in a document title.

Turning Documents Into Answers

Once documentation exists, it becomes a source Claude can work from.

Using only the attached procedure documents, answer this question. If the documents don't contain the answer, say so instead of guessing.
Why This Matters

This pattern is useful for new-hire questions, and it also exposes gaps. Every question the documents can't answer is a documentation task waiting to be done.

4 How Do You Keep Documentation From Going Stale?

Writing the document is the easy half. The harder half is making sure it is still true six months later.

Give Every Document an Owner and a Review Date

Add a header block to this document with: owner, last reviewed date, next review date, and the systems or tools this document depends on.

The dependency list is quietly powerful. When a tool changes, every document that mentions it can be found and checked.

Refresh Instead of Rewrite

When something changes, rebuilding the document from scratch is rarely necessary.

Here is our current procedure and a description of what changed in the process. Update only the affected steps, keep everything else identical, and show me a list of exactly what you changed.

The change list lets a reviewer approve the update in a minute instead of re-reading everything.

Run a Periodic Audit

Review this set of documents and flag any that reference tools, roles, or dates that may be outdated, any that contradict each other, and any that have no clear owner.
💡

Schedule it quarterly: A short audit is far cheaper than a failed process caused by a stale instruction.

5 What Are the Most Common Documentation Mistakes?

The same five mistakes account for most documentation that ends up ignored or wrong.

✕

Writing documentation once and never reviewing it. Without an owner and a review date, every document is on a slow path to being wrong.

✕

Letting Claude fill gaps with guesses. Always instruct it to flag unclear points rather than invent details.

✕

Writing for yourself instead of the reader. Define the audience, and test the procedure as a first-time user.

✕

Building a deep, clever folder structure. If people can't find a document quickly, they stop using the system.

✕

Trusting documentation without verifying it against reality. Have someone actually follow the procedure once before publishing it.

Key Takeaways
  • Start from raw material, not a blank page — notes, threads, and interviews are the input
  • Tell Claude not to invent, and to list what's unclear — gaps become a follow-up list instead of silent guesses
  • Let Claude interview the expert — the obvious steps experts skip are the ones newcomers need
  • Define the reader first, and include an expected result for every step
  • Keep the knowledge base shallow and searchable in the words people actually use
  • Every document needs an owner, a review date, and a dependency list
  • Update only what changed, list the changes, and audit quarterly
Practice Exercises

Foundation Option

Pick one process only you know well. Use the interview prompt to have Claude question you about it, then turn the result into a structured procedure.

Advanced Option

Take five existing documents from a team and run them through the audit prompt. List which ones are outdated, overlapping, or ownerless, and draft a simple structure and review schedule for them.

Reflection Questions

Which step was skipped in the explanation because it felt too obvious? Which current documents would be hard to trust today? Who should own each key document, and when will it next be reviewed?
