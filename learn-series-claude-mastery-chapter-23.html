Process Optimization

Find the Friction, Then Design It Away

⏱️ 12 min read ◆ 📊 Advanced ◆ 🎯 Process Optimization

  • 3. Redesigning Without Complexity

Every team has at least one process that everyone quietly hates. The approval chain that takes eight days for a decision that should take one. The report rebuilt from scratch every month because nobody documented the last version. The handoff between two departments that loses information every single time. These processes rarely get fixed because fixing them requires seeing the whole workflow clearly and questioning steps that "have always been done that way" — and that's exactly where Claude is useful.

Quick Answer

Using Claude for process optimization means treating it as a neutral outside observer that maps a workflow exactly as described, asks the obvious questions nobody in the room wants to ask, and separates a bottleneck's visible symptom from its actual root cause. Because Claude doesn't know internal politics or an org chart's sensitivities, it will ask "why does this step exist at all?" — and redesign with subtraction, not addition, as the default instinct.

This chapter covers how to use Claude to map an existing workflow end-to-end, find bottlenecks and their real root cause, redesign a process without adding new complexity, and pressure-test a redesign before rolling it out.

1 How Do You Map a Process the Way It Actually Runs?

The biggest mistake in process improvement is optimizing the process you think you have instead of the one that actually exists. Documentation is almost always stale. The real process includes workarounds, exceptions, and a person named "just check with Dave" that never made it into any manual.

Getting the Real Map Out of Your Head

Describing a messy process out loud, to something that will ask clarifying questions, forces gaps to surface that would otherwise get papered over.

I'm going to describe our current invoice approval process from memory. As I describe it, ask me clarifying questions about any step that seems vague, any step where you're not sure who's responsible, or any step that seems to exist only as a workaround. Here's the process: [describe every step you can think of, including who does it and roughly how long it takes]
Key Insight

This single instruction — ask questions where things are vague — does more to surface the real process than trying to document it perfectly yourself first.

Turning the Description Into a Structured Map

Once the raw description exists, ask Claude to structure it — and to flag anywhere it had to guess.

Turn this into a structured process map: for each step, list the owner, the input required, the output produced, the average time it takes, and whether it's a decision point or a straight pass-through. Flag any step where you had to guess or where I gave you incomplete information.

That last flag is important — it shows exactly where your own understanding of the process has gaps, which is often the first real finding of the whole exercise.

2 How Do You Find the Real Root Cause of a Bottleneck?

A bottleneck is where things slow down. The cause of the bottleneck is usually somewhere else entirely. Fixing the visible symptom without finding the cause is why the same process gets "streamlined" every year and never actually gets faster.

Distinguishing Symptom From Cause

Looking at this process map, identify the 3 biggest sources of delay. For each one, don't just describe where the delay happens — ask me questions to help identify why it happens. Is it a resourcing issue, an unclear decision-making authority, a missing piece of information, a dependency on someone outside the process, or something else?
💡

Five whys, applied to a workflow: This is where Claude's willingness to keep asking "why" past the first answer earns its place — the same technique normally applied to an incident, applied instead to a process.

Quantifying the Real Cost

Bottlenecks are easier to justify fixing when the cost is explicit.

Based on this process map, estimate the cumulative time cost of the current bottlenecks over a typical month, assuming [X] instances of this process run per month. Present this as a simple before/after comparison table I could use to justify the redesign to leadership.

3 How Do You Redesign a Process Without Adding Complexity?

The natural instinct when fixing a broken process is to add a step: another approval, another check, another form. This usually creates a new bottleneck to replace the old one.

The Subtraction-First Approach

Before suggesting any new steps, review this process map and identify: 1) any step that could be removed entirely without meaningful risk, 2) any two steps that could be merged, 3) any step that could be automated rather than done manually. Only after that, suggest what (if anything) needs to be added.

Forcing subtraction first, before addition, tends to produce leaner redesigns — and it mirrors how the best real-world process improvements actually work.

Step 1
✂️
Remove
Any step that adds no meaningful risk protection if cut entirely
Step 2
🔗
Merge
Two steps that exist separately but could run as one
Step 3
⚙️
Automate
Manual work that a tool or rule could reliably handle instead
Step 4 — Last Resort
➕
Add
Only what's still genuinely missing after the first three passes

Designing for the Exception, Not Just the Common Case

A process redesigned only around the happy path breaks the first time an edge case appears — and then everyone reverts to the old way "just this once," which quietly becomes permanent.

For this redesigned process, walk through 3 realistic exception cases (a rush request, a missing approver, a request that needs to be reversed). For each, tell me whether the new process handles it cleanly or whether it needs an explicit exception path.

4 How Do You Pressure-Test a Redesigned Process Before Rollout?

A redesign that looks clean on paper can still fail in practice because it assumes behavior that people won't actually adopt, or because it depends on a tool or approval that isn't reliably available.

Playing Out the Rollout

Walk through what the first week of this new process would actually look like from the perspective of someone doing it for the first time, with no extra training beyond a short announcement. Where would they get confused? Where would they likely fall back to the old habit?

Building in a Feedback Loop

A redesigned process without a way to catch new problems just becomes tomorrow's undocumented workaround.

Suggest one lightweight way to collect feedback on this new process during its first month, without adding meaningful overhead to the people using it.
Why This Matters

The redesign isn't finished at rollout — it's finished once there's a way to notice what the redesign missed. A process without a feedback mechanism just delays the next undocumented workaround by a few months.

5 What Are the Most Common Process Optimization Mistakes?

The same five mistakes account for most failed process redesigns, regardless of the process or the team running it.

✕

Mapping the process you assume exists, not the one that actually runs. Always start from a real, current description — not the documented version.

✕

Fixing the visible delay instead of its root cause. A bottleneck two steps upstream is often the real problem.

✕

Adding a new step to fix a broken one. Look for what can be removed or merged before adding anything.

✕

Designing only for the common case. Exceptions are where redesigned processes usually break first.

✕

Rolling out a new process with no feedback mechanism. Without one, the new problems just become the next generation of undocumented workarounds.

◆ ◆ ◆
Key Takeaways
  • Map the real process, not the documented one — describe it out loud and let Claude question the vague parts
  • A bottleneck's visible location is rarely its root cause — keep asking why past the first answer
  • Subtract before you add — remove, merge, and automate before proposing anything new
  • Design for the exception cases early — that's where a redesign built only for the happy path breaks first
  • Pressure-test before rollout — walk through the first real use with no extra training
  • Every redesign needs a feedback loop, or its blind spots become tomorrow's workarounds
Practice Exercises

Foundation Option

Pick one process personally found frustrating and describe it out loud to Claude using the mapping prompt. Notice which steps were hardest to explain clearly — that's usually a sign the step is poorly defined even for the people doing it.

Advanced Option

Take a process map already available for a team, run it through the bottleneck and redesign prompts, and compare the subtraction-first redesign to what a typical "add a checkpoint" fix would have looked like.

Reflection Questions

Where did the real process differ from how it would have been described before this exercise? Which bottleneck's root cause was surprising? What did the subtraction-first redesign remove that wouldn't have been an obvious cut otherwise?
