AI-Human Collaboration Patterns

Know What to Hand Over and What to Keep

⏱️ 13 min read ◆ 📊 Mastery ◆ 🎯 Working With AI

By this point in the series you know how to prompt well. The next level of mastery is not another technique — it is a working relationship. Two people can use the same tool and get very different results: one treats Claude as a search box and gets generic answers; another hands over everything, including decisions that needed their judgment, and ships work they never really evaluated. Both are using Claude. Neither is collaborating with it.

Quick Answer

AI-human collaboration means deciding, task by task, what to delegate, what to work on together, and what to keep — then moving work back and forth through short draft-and-refine loops with specific feedback. Delegate effort, never accountability: use Claude for volume and first drafts, as a reviewer and challenger of finished work, and as a thinking partner, while the final judgment stays with you.

Real collaboration is closer to managing a very fast, very well-read colleague than to operating software. That colleague has no memory of yesterday unless context is provided, cannot see priorities unless they are stated, and will produce confident-sounding work whether or not it is right. This chapter covers how to divide the work, run the loop, use Claude to examine work, and build a rhythm that improves over time.

1 How Do You Decide What to Delegate to Claude and What to Keep?

Before writing a prompt, decide the division of labor. Most poor results trace back to a mismatch at this stage, not a weak prompt.

A Simple Sorting Test

For any task, three questions settle who does what.

1
Does it require context only I have?Office politics, a client's unspoken preferences, what happened last quarter.
If yesProvide the context, or keep the task.
2
Does it carry consequences I'm accountable for?A decision, a published claim, a message to a customer.
If yesYou make the final call.
3
Is it mostly volume, structure, or first-draft effort?Summarizing, reformatting, drafting options, checking for gaps.
If yesDelegate it freely.

Three Modes of Working

The answers map onto three modes, each with a different balance of effort between Claude and the person.

Delegate
Claude does the work, you review the result.
ClaudeYou

Formatting, summaries, first drafts, data cleanup.

Collaborate
You and Claude work back and forth.
ClaudeYou

Strategy, writing with a point of view, problem-solving.

Retain
You do it, with Claude as a sounding board.
ClaudeYou

Final decisions, sensitive conversations, anything where your judgment is the point.

I'm deciding whether to restructure my team's reporting process. I'll make the decision myself. Your role is to help me think: list the strongest arguments for and against, ask me the questions I should be asking, and point out anything I seem to be assuming. Don't recommend a decision.
Key Insight

Stating the role up front keeps Claude from jumping to a conclusion that wasn't asked for. The same question gets a different, more useful answer when the mode is named.

2 How Does a Draft-and-Refine Loop Work?

The best results rarely come from a single prompt. They come from a short loop where each round makes the work more specific to the person doing it.

The Basic Loop

1
Brief
Goal, audience, constraints, what good looks like
You
2
Draft
A first version
Claude
3
React
Specific feedback, not "make it better"
You
4
Refine
Revises only what was pointed to
Claude
5
Decide
Judge whether it is ready
You

Giving Feedback That Moves Work Forward

Vague feedback gets vague changes. Naming what to keep is as important as naming what to change — without it, a revision can quietly undo parts that were working.

❌ Weak Feedback
"This doesn't feel right."

Produces change without direction — Claude has to guess what "right" means.

✅ Strong Feedback
"Opening is too formal. Keep paragraph two exactly as is. Cut paragraph three to two sentences and make the call to action more direct."

Names the problem, the part to protect, and the change wanted.

Knowing When to Stop

💡

The third-revision rule: If the third revision is not clearly better than the second, the problem is usually the brief, not the draft. Step back and rewrite the brief instead of polishing further.

3 How Can Claude Act as Reviewer, Challenger, and Thinking Partner?

Many people use Claude only to produce. Some of its most valuable uses are to examine work that already exists.

Reviewer
Examine finished work from a named viewpoint

Assigning a specific viewpoint makes the review sharper than a general request for feedback.

Review this proposal as a skeptical finance director. List the three weakest points, the questions I'm most likely to be asked, and anything that sounds like an unsupported claim.
Challenger
Argue against the plan, on request

A challenger is useful precisely because it has no stake in being agreeable. Ask for disagreement explicitly, because the default tone leans cooperative.

Here is my plan. Argue against it as strongly as you can. Then tell me which of your objections you think is most serious and why.
Thinking Partner
Ask better questions than the ones already being asked

Sometimes the most useful thing Claude can do is not solve the problem yet.

I'm stuck on how to approach this. Don't solve it yet. Ask me five questions that would help me clarify what I actually want.

4 How Do You Build a Collaboration Rhythm That Gets Better?

Collaboration becomes powerful when it stops being improvised each time.

Keep Your Context Reusable

Write down the background that keeps getting re-explained: role, audience, style, standards. Keep it in a short note to paste at the start of a session, or in a project where it persists. Repeating it costs minutes; not having it costs consistency.

Capture What Works

When a prompt gives excellent results, save it with a note on when to use it. Over a few weeks that becomes a personal library more valuable than any generic list.

Review Your Own Habits

Once a month, look back at recent work and ask where delegation is off in either direction.

Based on these examples of how I've used Claude, where am I doing work that could be delegated? Where am I delegating something I should be reviewing more closely?
Why This Matters

Both errors are common, and they point in opposite directions: doing too much by hand, and trusting too much without checking. A monthly look back catches whichever one is drifting.

5 What Are the Most Common Collaboration Mistakes?

The same five mistakes account for most weak results from working with Claude, whatever the task.

✕

Handing over judgment along with the work. Delegate effort, not accountability. You own the result.

✕

Doing everything yourself out of habit. Volume, formatting, and first drafts rarely need your direct effort.

✕

Vague feedback loops. "Make it better" produces change without improvement. Be specific about what to keep and what to change.

✕

Never asking Claude to disagree. If every response feels agreeable, the challenge needed probably isn't arriving.

✕

Re-explaining context every time. Capture it once and reuse it.

◆ ◆ ◆
Key Takeaways
  • Decide the division of labor before writing the prompt — most poor results start with a mismatch there
  • Use the three-question test: private context, accountability, or volume and first-draft effort
  • Name the mode up front — delegate, collaborate, or retain — and state Claude's role
  • Run short loops: brief, draft, react, refine, decide — and rewrite the brief if round three isn't better than round two
  • Give feedback that names what to keep as well as what to change
  • Use Claude to review, challenge, and ask questions — and ask for disagreement explicitly
  • Save reusable context and winning prompts, and audit your own delegation habits monthly
Practice Exercises

Foundation Option

Take three tasks from the coming week and sort each into Delegate, Collaborate, or Retain using the three-question test. Run one of them with the role stated up front.

Advanced Option

Take a piece of work already finished and run it through the Reviewer and Challenger prompts. Note which objections had not been considered.

Reflection Questions

Which kind of task tends to be over-delegated, and which over-kept? Where has vague feedback cost extra rounds of revision? What background would be worth saving to stop re-explaining it?

Frequently Asked Questions

Ask three questions about each task. Does it require context only you have? If so, provide that context or keep the task. Does it carry consequences you are accountable for, such as a decision or a message to a customer? If so, you make the final call. Is it mostly volume, structure, or first-draft effort? If so, delegate it freely.

Delegating means Claude does the work and you review the result, which suits formatting, summaries, and first drafts. Collaborating means working back and forth, which suits strategy and writing with a point of view. Retaining means you do the task yourself, perhaps with Claude as a sounding board, which suits final decisions and sensitive conversations.

A draft-and-refine loop is a short cycle of five steps: you brief the goal, audience, and constraints; Claude drafts; you react with specific feedback; Claude revises only what you pointed to; and you decide whether the work is ready. If the third revision is not clearly better than the second, the problem is usually the brief, so rewrite it instead of polishing further.

Be specific about what to change and what to keep. A comment like "make it better" produces change without improvement, while naming the exact problem, the parts to leave untouched, and the desired adjustment gives a targeted revision. Naming what to keep matters because a revision can otherwise quietly undo parts you liked.

Assign a specific viewpoint and ask for disagreement explicitly. For example, ask Claude to review a proposal as a skeptical finance director and list the weakest points and likely questions, or to argue against your plan as strongly as possible and name the most serious objection. The default tone leans cooperative, so the request for challenge has to be stated.
