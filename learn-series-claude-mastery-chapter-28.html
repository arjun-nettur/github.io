Measuring & Optimizing AI Impact

Prove It's Working, Then Make It Work Better

⏱️ 13 min read ◆ 📊 Mastery ◆ 🎯 Measurement

  • 2. Capturing a Baseline
  • 3. Tracking & Friction

Ask most people whether AI is saving them time and they will say yes. Ask how much, and the answer gets vague: "a lot," "hours every week," "it's just faster." That feeling may well be accurate. But a feeling is not evidence, and it is exposed in two ways. Without measurement, you never learn which uses are paying off and which are quietly wasting effort. And when a manager, client, or budget holder asks for a justification, "it feels faster" is a weak answer.

Quick Answer

To measure AI impact, pick one or two outcome metrics per task (time, quality, errors, or capacity), capture a baseline before changing anything, and count the whole cost of the AI-assisted version, including prompting, reviewing, fixing, and restarting. Track results for a few weeks, change one variable at a time, and report the sample size and at least one limitation.

There is also a subtler problem. AI can make work feel faster while moving the effort somewhere else. A draft that takes two minutes to generate but forty minutes to fix is not a time saving, and a report produced in half the time but with more errors is not an improvement. Only measurement shows the difference. This chapter covers what to measure, how to set a baseline, how to track results and spot friction, and how to optimize and report honestly.

1 What Should You Measure to Know Whether AI Is Working?

The first mistake in measurement is picking whatever is easiest to count. Prompts sent, hours spent in the tool, and documents generated are all easy to count, and none of them shows whether the work got better.

Four Categories Worth Tracking

⏱️ Time

How long the task takes from start to finished, including review and corrections.

✅ Quality

How good the result is, judged against a standard defined in advance.

⚠️ Errors

How often the output needs correction, and how serious the corrections are.

📈 Capacity

How much more can be handled, or what can now be done that could not before.

💡

Pick one or two per task, not all four. A measurement system that takes longer than the task it measures will not survive a week.

Outcomes Over Activity

Activity
  • "I used Claude for 12 reports this month."

Shows only that you were busy.

Outcome
  • "Report turnaround dropped from three days to one, and revision rounds stayed the same."

Shows whether the effort was worthwhile.

Define "Good" Before You Start

Quality is the hardest to measure, so decide the standard up front. Yes-or-no criteria turn a subjective impression into something that can be counted.

Help me write a short checklist for what a good customer summary looks like, with five yes-or-no criteria I can apply to each one. Make the criteria specific enough that two different people would score the same summary the same way.

2 How Do You Capture a Baseline Before Changing Anything?

A result means nothing without something to compare it to. The baseline is how the task performed before AI, or before the approach changed.

Measure the Before

Pick a task done regularly and record, for a handful of instances: how long it took start to finish, how many revision rounds it needed, and any errors found afterward. Five to ten examples is enough to see a pattern. If Claude has been in use for a while, reconstruct a rough baseline from file dates, email timestamps, and personal estimates, labelled as estimates.

Be Honest About the Whole Cost

When measuring the AI-assisted version, include everything. Counting only generation time is the most common way to overstate savings.

Keep Conditions Comparable

If the AI-assisted tasks were the easy ones and the baseline tasks were the hard ones, the comparison is unfair. Compare similar tasks, or note the difference when reporting.

Ask Claude to Help Analyze

Here is my log of 15 tasks with time taken, revision rounds, and errors. Identify which task types show the biggest improvement, which show little or none, and which show a worse result. Don't speculate beyond the data. If there isn't enough data to conclude something, say so.

The final instruction matters. With a small sample, an honest "not enough data" is more useful than a confident but unsupported conclusion.

Where Friction Hides

Heavy rework

The first output always needs major changes.

Suggests: a weak prompt or brief

Verification overhead

Checking takes nearly as long as doing the task.

Suggests: this task may not suit AI

Context re-entry

Large amounts of time spent re-explaining background.

Suggests: save reusable context

Quality drift

Speed improves but errors or complaints creep up.

Suggests: tighten review and standards

Key Insight

A task where AI does not help is a valid finding. Stopping use there is an optimization, not a failure.

4 How Do You Optimize and Report Results Credibly?

Improve One Variable at a Time

When a task underperforms, change one thing, then measure again: rewrite the brief with clearer constraints, add an example of the output wanted, split one big request into two steps, or save the context that keeps getting retyped. If everything changes at once, there is no way to know what worked.

Here are three prompts I've used for the same task and the results each produced. What differences between the prompts might explain the difference in results? Suggest one revision to test.

Reporting Results Credibly

When reporting to a manager or client, keep it short and honest.

What was measured and whyName the metric and the decision it supports.
Baseline versus currentState the sample size next to every comparison.
What improved, what didn't, what got worseInclude the unflattering results as well.
What changes nextOne variable to test in the next round.
Draft a half-page summary of these results for a non-technical manager. State the sample size, include one limitation, and avoid claiming more than the data supports.

Reporting the limits of the data builds trust. Claims that are too good tend to collapse the first time someone checks them.

5 What Are the Most Common Measurement Mistakes?

✕

No baseline. Without a before, any number is just a number.

✕

Measuring activity instead of outcomes. Prompts sent and hours logged show effort, not results.

✕

Counting only generation time. Include prompting, reviewing, correcting, and restarting.

✕

Chasing vanity metrics. If a number looks good but changes no decision, drop it.

✕

Overclaiming from small samples. Ten examples show a trend, not proof. Say so.

Key Takeaways
  • A feeling of speed is not evidence; measure to learn which uses pay off
  • Track outcomes (time, quality, errors, capacity), not activity, and pick one or two per task
  • Define "good" as yes-or-no criteria before starting
  • Capture a baseline from five to ten instances, labelling reconstructed numbers as estimates
  • Count the whole cost: prompting, reviewing, fixing, and restarting
  • Change one variable at a time, and treat "AI doesn't help here" as a valid result
  • Report sample size and at least one limitation, and claim no more than the data supports
Practice Exercises

Foundation Option

Choose one recurring task. Record time, revision rounds, and errors for five instances without AI help or from past work, then for five with Claude. Compare the two.

Advanced Option

Run a full cycle on one workflow: define a yes-or-no quality checklist, capture a baseline, track four weeks of results, change one variable, and write a half-page report with a stated limitation.

Reflection Questions

Which AI uses are believed to save time but have never been measured? Where might effort have shifted into review rather than disappeared? What claim about AI results would be hard to defend if challenged?
